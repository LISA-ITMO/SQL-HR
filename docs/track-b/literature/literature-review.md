# Evidence-based competency assessment

## Introduction

The growing adoption of artificial intelligence is changing the skills required in many occupations and contributing to the transformation of professional roles [1]. These changes make it increasingly important to describe professional requirements more precisely than job titles alone allow. Competency frameworks, such as the Skills Framework for the Information Age (SFIA 9) [18], provide structured descriptions of professional competencies and proficiency levels that can support the specification of role requirements. However, defining the competencies required for a role does not necessarily establish how these requirements should be used to assess an individual candidate.

Previous research has addressed several aspects of competency representation and candidate assessment. Ontology-based approaches have modeled competencies, proficiency levels, supporting evidence, and provenance [2, 3]. More recently, Lê et al. [4] proposed linking educational text fragments to competencies using traceable evidence and graph constraints. In recruitment, person-job fit methods have considered individual job requirements and candidate abilities [6, 7], while JobMatchAI [5] combines semantic retrieval, structured scoring, and grounded explanations. Du et al. [10] have also explored the use of large language models for competency modeling based on interview evidence. Although these studies provide important foundations, they address different aspects of competency modeling, evidence alignment, and candidate matching.

A remaining challenge is how to translate competency-based role requirements into reproducible assessments of an individual's demonstrated proficiency. Identifying a correspondence between a candidate's experience and a competency does not necessarily establish the level of proficiency supported by that experience. For example, a project report may mention a particular technology without demonstrating the candidate's contribution, degree of autonomy, or ability to perform the relevant tasks. Furthermore, evidence supporting a level below the role requirement must be distinguished from evidence that is insufficient to determine any level. This raises the question of how heterogeneous evidence can be evaluated against competency indicators and proficiency-level descriptions while preserving uncertainty and the traceability of assessment decisions.

To address this problem, this paper proposes a procedure for evidence-grounded candidate assessment based on competency-role models. The proposed approach connects role-specific requirements with competency indicators and target proficiency levels, and aligns documented evidence from projects, portfolios, and other candidate records with these requirements. It aims to determine which proficiency levels are supported by the available evidence while distinguishing demonstrated competency gaps from insufficient information. The resulting assessments retain references to supporting evidence, providing a basis for interpretable candidate comparisons, competency-gap analysis, and explainable ranking.

The study aims to investigate how evidence-grounded competency assessments can support person-job fit evaluation and how the resulting assessments and explanations affect candidate ranking and selection decisions. The evaluation examined agreement with expert judgments, the traceability of supporting evidence, and the effects of explicitly representing proficiency gaps and uncertainty on candidate comparisons and selection decisions. The contributions of this study are a reproducible procedure for connecting competency-based role requirements with documented candidate evidence, an empirical assessment of its influence on ranking and candidate selection, and insights into explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) for person-job fit by making the evidence and reasoning behind candidate assessments more transparent and verifiable.

## Related Works

Competency frameworks provide structured representations of professional requirements, although their purposes and granularity vary. SFIA 9 [18] specifies professional skills across seven levels of responsibility, while the European e-Competence Framework (e-CF) [21] describes information and communication technology (ICT) competencies and associated proficiency levels. European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations (ESCO) [19] and the Occupational Information Network (O*NET) [20] organize occupations and related skill or work descriptors. More specialized competency-role models, including ITMO University’s competency-role model (KRM) [16] and the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (MIPT) competency-role materials [17], describe professional requirements through competencies, indicators, and proficiency levels. These resources provide reference structures for specifying role requirements, but applying them to individual candidate assessment requires decisions about how available evidence should be interpreted.

Evidence-based competency assessment has been investigated in ontology research. Dorn et al. [2] developed a human resources (HR) ontology connecting occupations, competency grades, and supporting evidence, including mechanisms for representing competency evaluation. Paquette et al. [3] proposed a competency ontology incorporating proficiency levels, evidence records, portfolios, confidence ratings, and provenance information. Their framework also supports competency comparisons for personalized learning-resource recommendations. More recently, Lê et al. [4] combined large language models with graph constraints to associate educational text fragments with competencies while preserving traceable evidence. Du et al. [10] explored competency modeling from interview data using large language models and evaluated the resulting models against performance-related outcomes. These studies establish important approaches to competency representation, evidence management, and assessment. However, their objectives differ: representing competency evidence, identifying relevant text fragments, and evaluating interview-derived competency models are not equivalent to determining whether a candidate's documented experience supports a specific proficiency level required by a professional role.

Research on person-job fit has developed methods for matching candidate profiles with job requirements. Zhu et al. [6] proposed the Person-Job Fit Neural Network (PJFNN), which models correspondence between candidates and individual job requirements, while Qin et al. [7] introduced the Ability-aware Person-Job Fit Neural Network (APJFNN) to relate candidate experiences to ability requirements. TAROT [11] further incorporates the hierarchical structure of resumes and job descriptions into representation learning. Knowledge-graph-based approaches have also been investigated for talent recommendation and HR information extraction [8, 9]. More recently, JobMatchAI [5] combined semantic and graph-based retrieval, constraint filtering, factor-based ranking, and grounded explanations to support candidate matching. Its evaluation also includes a pilot user study examining the perceived usefulness of explanations. These approaches demonstrate that person-job fit systems can incorporate requirement-level comparisons, structured representations, and explainable ranking mechanisms. Nevertheless, identifying a match between candidate experience and a job requirement does not necessarily establish which proficiency level is demonstrated by the available evidence. Similarly, an explanation grounded in candidate information does not by itself establish whether that information is sufficient to justify a particular competency assessment.

Constraint-based and knowledge-based recommendation methods provide another relevant foundation for candidate ranking and selection. Felfernig and Burke [12] and Uta et al. [13] examine approaches that incorporate explicit requirements, preferences, and constraints into recommendation processes. Amsterdamer et al. [14] further investigate declarative user selection with soft constraints. These studies establish methods for handling requirements and preferences during selection and ranking. However, their primary focus differs from inferring demonstrated proficiency levels from candidate evidence, which is necessary when ranking criteria depend on demonstrated rather than merely reported abilities.

Uncertainty handling is also relevant to evidence-grounded assessment. Wen et al. [15] review abstention mechanisms in large language models, while Won et al. [22] investigate the distinction between missing evidence and distracting information in retrieval-augmented language models. These studies emphasize the importance of accounting for insufficient or unreliable information when generating conclusions. Unlike abstention in question answering, evidence-grounded candidate assessment requires distinguishing between evidence supporting a proficiency level below the role requirement and insufficient evidence to determine a candidate's level. This distinction may have implications for candidate comparison and ranking.

Taken together, existing research provides foundations for competency representation, evidence-based assessment, person-job fit, explainable ranking, and constraint-based recommendation. However, an open methodological question concerns how heterogeneous candidate evidence can be systematically evaluated against role-specific competency indicators and proficiency requirements while distinguishing demonstrated proficiency gaps from insufficient information. It also remains important to investigate how such evidence-grounded assessments affect candidate ranking and how their explanations support the interpretation of candidate comparisons and selection decisions. Addressing these questions requires empirical evaluation of agreement with expert judgments, evidence traceability, ranking quality, and explanation usefulness.

## Methods

### Study Design and Data

This study was designed as a pilot evaluation of an evidence-grounded candidate assessment method based on competency-role models. ITMO KRM [16] was used as the primary reference framework because it provides structured descriptions of professional roles, competencies, indicators, and proficiency levels. The MIPT competency-role materials [17] were also considered as an additional reference for examining the applicability of the procedure to another competency-role model.

The dataset combined three sources: publicly available candidate profiles collected from online recruitment platforms and subsequently de-identified, synthetically generated candidate profiles, and de-identified records of students working in a research laboratory at ITMO University. The data included cv’s, project descriptions, portfolios, and documented achievements.

The pilot study included three to five professional roles and approximately 20-30 candidate profiles per role. Two domain experts participated in the assessment. Personal information in the real candidate records was anonymized before evaluation.

### Assessment Procedure

The assessment began with the selection of professional roles and their competency requirements from the reference model. For each role, we identified the relevant competencies, their indicators, and the required proficiency levels. Where the model did not specify target levels for individual indicators, these were defined using assessment guidelines agreed upon with domain experts.

Candidate profiles were then analyzed to identify evidence of relevant skills and experience, including descriptions of completed projects, specific responsibilities, technical tasks, and achieved results. Each relevant fragment was linked to a competency indicator, with a reference to the original document. For example, mentioning a programming language in a résumé does not necessarily demonstrate proficiency unless the candidate’s actual experience with it is described.

The identified evidence was compared with the proficiency-level descriptions for each indicator. Based on this comparison, we determined whether the available information supported the required level, indicated a lower level, or was insufficient for a reliable assessment. This distinction allowed us to separate confirmed competency gaps from cases where information about the candidate was insufficient.

Finally, the indicator-level assessments were used to compare candidates against the requirements of each role. Candidate rankings were calculated based on demonstrated proficiency, identified competency gaps, and insufficient evidence. The resulting rankings were accompanied by explanations referring to the specific evidence used in the assessments.

### Evaluation and Data Analysis

The method was evaluated through expert annotation, assessment by LLM-as-a-Judge, and comparison with simpler matching approaches. Two domain experts independently assessed candidate evidence and assigned proficiency levels to the relevant indicators. In parallel, a large language model was used as an automated judge to evaluate the same evidence against the competency indicators and proficiency-level descriptions. Disagreements between human experts were discussed to establish a consensus reference.

Inter-annotator agreement was measured using appropriate agreement statistics, including ordinal Krippendorff's alpha for proficiency levels. Agreement between LLM-as-a-Judge assessments and expert consensus was also evaluated. Candidate rankings were compared with expert judgments and rankings produced by baseline methods, including keyword-based matching and competency-level matching without indicator granularity.
