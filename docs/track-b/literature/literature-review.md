# Evidence-based competency assessment

## Introduction

The rapid development of artificial intelligence is reshaping the labor market, creating new professional specializations and changing the skills required for existing roles [1]. As a result, the differences between related job positions may not always be clear from job titles and descriptions alone. Competency-role models can help address this problem by providing structured descriptions of professional roles, competencies, observable indicators, and proficiency levels. However, using these models to assess individual candidates requires additional decisions about what constitutes reliable evidence of competence and how uncertainty should be handled.

Prior research has connected competencies with evidence records, portfolios, and confidence ratings [2, 3], linked textual fragments to competency graphs [4], and developed explainable job-matching systems [5]. Building on these approaches, this study proposes a procedure for evidence-based competency assessment. The proposed method aims to link evidence from individual projects, participation records, and achievements to competency indicators, determine the proficiency levels supported by this evidence, and compare the results with role-specific requirements. It distinguishes between evidence supporting the required proficiency level, evidence supporting only a lower level, and insufficient evidence (UNKNOWN), without treating missing evidence as missing ability. A project report may mention a technology without establishing the contributor’s action, autonomy, or attained proficiency. The assessment therefore needs to link each relevant artifact fragment to a versioned indicator and its behavioral anchor, recording the exact evidence location.

A planned pilot study will evaluate agreement with expert assessments, traceability of decisions, uncertainty, and the effects of these assessments on candidate ranking.

## Related Works

Competency frameworks provide different structures for describing professional requirements. In ITMO KRM 3.0 [16], role targets and indicator anchors are separate structures; the MIPT materials [17] attach levels and learning outcomes to indicators and provide assessment guidance. SFIA 9 [18] uses seven responsibility levels with professional skill descriptions. ESCO [19] provides occupational and skill or knowledge concepts, O*NET [20] organizes occupational descriptors, and e-CF [21] provides an ICT competence reference. These normative or descriptive resources are useful comparisons, but their labels and levels cannot be treated as a common measurement scale. Any comparison must retain the source, version, and meaning of each level.

Evidence-oriented competency modelling has a longer history than current graph-based recruitment systems. Dorn et al. [2] represented occupations, competency grades, and supporting evidence in an HR ontology; Paquette et al. [3] extended competency ontologies with evidence records, portfolios, confidence ratings, and referential provenance. More recently, Lê et al. [4] aligned educational text fragments with competencies using verifiable spans and graph constraints. These studies establish important components of the proposed approach, although evidence-to-competency alignment does not automatically determine whether a candidate satisfies an ordinal, role-specific indicator.

In recruitment, JobMatchAI [5] combines hybrid retrieval, hard filters, deterministic scoring, and grounded explanations. PJFNN [6] estimates matches for individual job requirements, APJFNN [7] relates experience to ability requirements, and the publisher abstract of Yang and Shen [8] describes graph-based talent recommendation. HRGraph [9] extracts HR entities and relations from documents, while the preprint by Du et al. [10] maps interview evidence to competency libraries and validates predictions against performance groups. TAROT [11] models the hierarchical structure of resumes and jobs. Thus, prior person–job fit research cannot be dismissed as purely keyword-based or lacking interpretable components.

Constraint reasoning is also well established. Felfernig and Burke [12] and Uta et al. [13] survey constraint-based and knowledge-based recommendations, while Amsterdamer et al. [14] implement declarative user selection with soft constraints.

Research on language-model abstention [15] further motivates withholding unsupported conclusions, although this differs from marking a candidate indicator as UNKNOWN. Won et al. [22] distinguish missing evidence from distracting material in retrieval-augmented answering. This distinction motivates recording why an assessment remains UNKNOWN, but question-answering abstention does not itself establish competency-assessment validity. A mechanically valid citation also does not establish that the cited fragment is semantically sufficient to support the target level [4].

Across these distinct tasks, a narrower open question is how a documented rubric should combine heterogeneous, time-bounded evidence with role-specific target levels for indicators defined in a competency-role model, and whether separating confirmed lower-level support from genuinely insufficient evidence changes expert agreement and ranking behavior. This proposed combination requires empirical testing; its individual components are not claimed as new. Learning-resource tagging, recruitment matching, and assessment of an individual’s demonstrated indicator level have different targets, so their published scores cannot be compared directly. No exhaustive search establishing that this combination has never been studied has been completed.
